When Your AI
Can't Be Trusted

Companies plan for systems that crash. The new risk is an AI that keeps running - and keeps acting - after you've stopped trusting it.

The Problem

Nothing is down. That is the problem.

Monday, 9:12. The security team spots something wrong with the AI purchasing agent - software that orders goods and pays suppliers on its own, without waiting for a person. Someone may have tricked it, for example with hidden instructions in a supplier's email. It has already placed orders and passed tasks to other AI agents. And every system on the dashboard is still green.

That is what makes this hard. Business continuity plans - the playbook for keeping a company working in a crisis - are built for things that break: a server, a building, a supplier. Here nothing broke. The AI works fine. You just can't trust what it's doing. Few organizations are ready for that: 78% build their recovery around restoring systems, and 56% say they are not well prepared to stop unintended actions by AI agents.1

Editorial #3 asked whether to trust a single AI action. This one asks what to do when you can't trust the AI itself.

Your AI doesn't have to go offline for your business to be in crisis.

The new continuity problem
The Prevailing Approach

Today's advice covers AI that breaks - not AI that misbehaves

Most published advice on AI agents, including from the big cloud and AI companies, focuses on three things:2

Keep it running
Backup systems that take over if one fails.
Restore it
Bring the AI and its data back after a failure.
Prevent problems
Give the AI only the access it needs, and firm rules.
Still on, but untrusted?
The case almost no guidance covers.

The best of it adds a manual backup plan for when the AI is switched off. Very little covers the harder case: the AI is still on, still acting - and no longer trusted. The Cloud Security Alliance, an industry body, has called for recovery that restores trust, not just working systems.3

Many companies are building a "kill switch" - a button that shuts the AI down. It tells you how to stop the AI. It doesn't tell you who does its work afterwards, or when it's safe to turn it back on.
01 · Stop

Stop the agent, not the business

Switching every AI off stops the business with it. Leaving this one running lets the damage spread. What you need is a dial, not an on/off switch: decide in advance how much each AI may do without a person checking.

Full freedom
Works on its own, within agreed limits.
Limited
Important actions need a person's approval.
Paused
The AI is stopped. People do the work.
← Coming backFreedom is returned step by step, once it's proven safe - see "Trust again" below.
Measure
Time to Safe Mode
How long from spotting the problem to getting the AI under control - while the business keeps running.
Have ready
  • A plan for each critical process: what the AI may still do, when to pause it - and who makes the call.
  • A precise off switch: pause one AI without stopping the others - including tasks it already handed off.
02 · Take over

Someone has to do its job by morning

The AI is paused. But the 140 purchase orders it would have prepared today are still waiting. Who prepares them?

This is the continuity gap: the AI has stopped, and people haven't taken over yet. They may not be able to. If the work has run on AI for months, do people still have the access, the know-how and the time?

"A person already approves the AI's work" doesn't solve this. Approving 140 orders is not the same as preparing them.
Measure
Human Takeover Time
How long from deciding to switch to people until people are actually doing the work.
Have ready
  • A map of which business processes depend on which AI.
  • A backup team that has practised the work. A plan on paper is not a plan that works.
03 · Trust again

Fixed is not the same as trusted

The security hole is fixed. Everyone wants the AI back on. Wait.

Fixing the hole doesn't prove the AI is behaving. While it was compromised, someone may have changed its instructions or fed it false information. Check it properly - then give back its freedom step by step.

What counts as evidence5 checks
  1. Access. Its passwords and keys are replaced, and its access is cut back to what it really needs.
  2. Setup. Its instructions, settings and connected tools match a version you know is safe.
  3. Memory. Anything it learned or stored during the incident is cleared or checked.
  4. Behavior. It runs in "shadow mode": it works alongside people but can't act, and you compare its answers with theirs.
  5. Step-by-step return. First it can only look, then only draft for approval, then take small actions, then work freely. A named person signs off each step.

For the purchasing agent: two days in shadow mode, then drafts only, then small orders. Trust Restoration Time ends when the last step is signed off.

Measure
Trust Restoration Time
How long from pausing the AI until it's proven safe and approved to work on its own again.
Have ready
  • Return criteria agreed in advance: what proof you will need.
  • A step-by-step return: easy-to-undo actions first, money and data last.

Try it in 90 minutes

Pick one AI agent and pretend you can't trust it. Pause it, hand its work to people, and time each step. The three measures are proposals, not industry standards - this is how you test them.

Is it running?Can we trust it?

The question has moved from "is it running?" to "how much can we trust it to do?"

The real test is not whether an organization can restore its AI.

It's whether it can continue operating safely without it - and know when it's safe to trust it again.
